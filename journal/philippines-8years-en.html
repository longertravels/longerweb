PHILIPPINES · EIGHT YEARS LATER

The Same Sea,
8 Years Later

2018 → 2026 · Cebu — Bohol — Siquijor — Oslob — Moalboal · 9 min read

The same platform · Top: 2018 · Bottom: 2026

Eight years later, it was a group of four again — but I was the only one who came back to the Philippines, and stood below the same cliff-jump platform in Siquijor.

In February 2018, during Chinese New Year, I flew alone from Shanghai to Dumaguete. After two days wandering on my own, I met up with friends and did the small loop: Siquijor — Bohol — Oslob.

In August 2026, I flew from Shenzhen with a friend I'd met for the first time in the Maldives — the second time we ever met in person was this trip to the Philippines. In Cebu we joined friends from Beijing and Shanghai, and did the full clockwise loop: Cebu — Bohol — Siquijor — Oslob — Moalboal.

I went from the person who made a 100-page PDF itinerary for her first solo trip abroad, to someone whose plan is now a few lines in a phone note, half of it improvised.

That wasn't pure spontaneity — it was a lesson learned. Back then, the meticulous J-person made an airtight plan, then misread a ferry schedule and watched the whole itinerary collapse. Now I plan like a veteran: August is typhoon season and ferries get cancelled, so this time I booked only half the trip and left the other half to flexibility. And we got lucky — blue skies the entire way; the rain arrived the day after we left.

Sometimes it feels like nothing has changed. Sometimes it feels like everything has.

First, the sea: the Philippines ruins every other coastline

I didn't truly understand the sea until the Philippines. I'd just learned to swim in a pool, nearly drowned on my first trip to Thailand, and stayed away from deep water for years. Then, in the Philippines, words like "glass sea" and "jelly sea" suddenly became physical — clean, transparent, coral and fish right beneath your feet.

Even after seeing the Maldives, I still tell anyone who loves the ocean to see the Philippines first: for beginners, it's the gold standard.

The only downside: wherever you go afterwards, you'll quietly compare every coastline to this one.

About the language: just go, and it stops being a problem

Wherever I've travelled, people still ask me: what if my English is bad?

Eight years ago mine was terrible. I looked up most words filling in the arrival card, didn't know the word "reception", translated every menu with my camera — and once got into a red-faced bargaining argument with a local boatman because I couldn't tell "snorkeling" from "diving".

AI translation makes all of that easier today. But the real answer is: go first. Once you're out there, none of it is a problem.

Back then I met a man in his forties at a hostel whose entire English was "HI! THANK YOU" — and he'd travelled dozens of countries alone. I asked how he got through immigration. He said: "I just speak Chinese. If they don't understand, that's their problem. There's always a Chinese person, or someone, who'll help." We later ran into each other again in Shangri-La and at Meili Snow Mountain — he'd been to even more places.

On bargaining: I didn't push hard on the Siquijor tricycle day rate — the driver also looks after your things all day, and it's worth keeping the mood good on both sides. If you want to cut costs, buses, shared vans and renting a scooter (international licence required) drop the budget a lot.

Verdict: for value for money, the Philippines is first-tier in all of Southeast Asia.

Getting around

  • Between islands: mostly by ferry — bookable online or at the pier
  • In cities: Grab
  • Everywhere else: tricycles + private cars; scooters need an international licence

Stop by stop (this trip's highlights)

A playground for divers worldwide and a gentle ceiling for beginners. You won't run out of photo outfits here, and it's paradise if your plan is to do nothing at all.

Bohol

① Balicasag Island — about 30 minutes by boat from Panglao. Dolphins on the way out; freedivers can chase the jacks — a living wall of fish, made for dramatic underwater shots; wall diving for certified divers. Beginners and non-swimmers can snorkel the coral gardens — layered coral, clouds of small fish, plenty of turtles. Perfect for clear, natural underwater photos.

Balicasag Island · over the coral garden

② Napaling Point — on Bohol itself, a short ride away. A natural crack between rock walls, very cinematic, a must for divers. Shore entry: colourful coral and fish right away, richer walls further out.

Napaling Point · looking up from the crack

Moalboal

The sardines form a moving cloud over your head. Elsewhere you'd charter a boat to chase fish schools — here you just walk in from the shore. Beginner-friendly: turtles as soon as you get in, the sardine ball ten metres out. The canyoneering here is also a great summer option.

Sardine run · Moalboal

Oslob

Swimming with whale sharks from the shore. They're gentle here — lean against the boat edge and one will drift slowly past, nibbling brine shrimp.

Honestly, whale sharks weren't in the plan this time. My visit eight years ago left a bad taste: endless waiting, and water that reeked of dead fish. But my friend said this is the only place on earth where you can get this close to a whale shark this easily, and she didn't want to leave with regrets. Fine — we went.

Eight years on, the place has genuinely changed: fresh brine shrimp instead of scraps, cleaner water, and strict protection zones for the sharks.

The feeding controversy hasn't stopped — provisioning changes their migration patterns. I left disappointed eight years ago; eight years later I watched it getting better with my own eyes. The debate may continue, but this stretch of water is moving in the right direction. Some places you have to see before you're entitled to an opinion.

Siquijor — my favourite island

Snorkelling, beachcombing, chasing sunrises and sunsets, cliff jumping, rope swings into the spring lake, coconut groves, beach swings, old churches, giant trees, drone shots, cycling around the island — and a massage in the evening. Life doesn't get much better. You could happily do nothing here for a week.

Salagdoong · the jelly sea from above

Eight years ago I shot a veil photoshoot with my friend at Lazi Church; this time I brought her dress back for a sequel. In those eight years, we've unlocked a lot more map together.

Eight years ago, at the 10-metre platform, wearing a life jacket and feeling very brave, I jumped — hit the water, my legs went soft, and I landed in an undignified seat-first splash, bruised from hip to thigh for two weeks. This time I was going to redeem myself — but the tide was out, the water too shallow, the 10-metre platform closed. Only the 5-metre one was open.

As if the sky was saying: there aren't as many "next times" as you think. There is no "later".
